4.2 – Understanding Slope

In the previous lesson, you graphed straight lines by making a table of \(x\) and \(y\) values. Different equations made different graphs, but they were all linear — their points lined up perfectly.

In real-world situations, we often want to compare how fast things change. Some lines rise quickly, some fall, and others stay flat. That rate of change is called the slope — one of the most important ideas in algebra.

4.2.1 – What Is Slope?

Look at the four stairways above. They all go up and down, but not in the same way. Some are steep, some are gentle. Some rise as you move to the right, and others fall.

Lines on a graph work the same way. The slope tells how a line tilts — whether it goes up or down, and how steep it is.

If you walk along a line from left to right, two things happen: you move across the graph (that’s the run) and you move up or down (that’s the rise). Slope is how much the rise changes compared to the run.

Mathematicians use the letter m for slope — think of it as the measure of steepness:

\[ m = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} \]

Instead of the words rise and run, we often use the Greek letter Δ (delta), which means change. So:

  • Δy means “change in \(y\)” — how much the \(y\)-values go up or down.
  • Δx means “change in \(x\)” — how much the \(x\)-values move left or right.

That means the slope formula can also be written as:

\[ m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \]

A positive slope means the line rises as you move right. A negative slope means it falls. A larger slope means it’s steeper.

As you can see, each stair pattern has a different tilt — just like lines on a graph. Next, we’ll place these same stairs on a coordinate grid so we can measure their slope more precisely.

4.2.2 – Finding Slope from a Graph

Now let’s connect those stair patterns to a coordinate plane. This time, each set of stairs is overlaid with a grid so you can see the exact vertical and horizontal changes.

In each panel, count how far the person moves up or down (Δy) and right (Δx):

Panel Δy Δx Slope \(m=\tfrac{Δy}{Δx}\)
A +2 3 \(\tfrac{2}{3}\)
B –3 3 –1
C +3 3 1
D –2 3 –\(\tfrac{2}{3}\)

A positive slope means the line rises left-to-right. A negative slope means it falls. Notice that Panels A and C rise, while B and D fall — exactly what we saw before, now confirmed by the numbers.

Example — Count the changes

Pick two clear points on the line — here, \((0,2)\) and \((5,6)\).

Start at \((0,2)\) and count boxes to get to \((5,6)\):

  • Move right until you get to \(x=5\): \(\Delta x = 5\)
  • From there, move up until you reach \(y=6\): \(\Delta y = 4\)

Between these two points, the line moves right 5 and up 4. (In the next section, we’ll use these counts in the slope formula.)

Compute the slope of the line.

Counting from \((-6, 3)\) to \((3, -3)\) you move:

  • 9 to the right
  • 6 down

So \(\Delta x=9\), \(\Delta y=-6\), and \(m=-\tfrac{6}{9}=-\tfrac{2}{3}\).

4.2.3 – Interactive Slope Exploration

Sometimes the best way to learn about something is to play around with it. Try adjusting the two points below to see how slope changes.

The slope is zero because the \(y\)-values do not change (Δy = 0) for a horizontal line.

Try making a vertical line. You will notice that the slope is undefined. This is because the \(x\)-value doesn’t change, so Δx = 0. You can’t divide by zero, so the slope is undefined.

Straight up or straight down?

A vertical line has an undefined slope because you can’t divide by zero. But there is another way to think about it. Look at the picture below. One person is at the top of a cliff looking down at someone on the ground.

Is the cliff straight up, or is it straight down? It depends on who you ask! The person on the ground would say it is straight up. The person on top would say it is straight down. It can’t be both, and yet it is! Since it is both straight up and straight down, we say the slope is undefined.

4.2.4 – Finding Slope Using the Slope Formula

When a graph is available and the numbers are small, it’s often enough to count boxes. Sometimes the numbers are large or the points aren’t easy to plot. In those cases, we use the slope formula.

For the points \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\), the slope is:

\[ m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} \]

For example, the slope between the points \((1, 3)\) and \((4, 5)\) is:

\[ m = \frac{5 - 3}{4 - 1} = \frac{2}{3} \]

Example — Using the slope formula

Compute the slope of the line between the points \((-2, 8)\) and \((10, 6)\).

Here \(x_1 = -2\), \(x_2 = 10\), \(y_1 = 8\), and \(y_2 = 6\).

\[ \begin{align*} m &= \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} \\ &= \frac{6 - 8}{10 - (-2)} \\ &= \frac{-2}{12} \\ &= -\frac{1}{6} \end{align*} \]

\[ \begin{align*} m &= \frac{2 - 95}{-50 - 0} \\ &= \frac{-93}{-50} \\ &= \frac{93}{50} \end{align*} \]

Real-world slope problems:

  1. A ramp rises 3 inches for every 36 inches forward. What is the slope?

  2. A road climbs 200 feet over a horizontal distance of 1 mile (5280 ft). What is the slope?

  3. A plumber installs a drain pipe that drops ¼ inch for every 12 inches of length. What is the slope as a fraction?

  4. A line passes through the points (time = 1 hour, distance = 40 miles) and (time = 3 hours, distance = 100 miles). What is the slope, and what does it represent?

  5. A skier starts 1000 ft above the lodge and reaches 400 ft above it after skiing 3000 ft forward. What is the slope?

  6. A student graphs temperature over time: \((2,40°)\) and \((6,68°)\). What is the slope, and what does it tell you?
