4.2 – Understanding Slope

In the previous lesson, you learned how to create tables of values for a linear function to create a graph. Different linear functions produced different graphs but they all produced straight lines.

In real-world situations, it is often useful to compare functions by looking at their graphs.

In this lesson, you will learn how to describe the way that lines change as you move from left to right. This rate of change is called the slope, one of the most important concepts you will learn in algebra.

slope, rise, run, rate, horizontal, vertical

🔥 Warm-Up

  1. Plot the function \(f(x) = 2x + 1\).

  2. On the same graph, plot the function \(g(x) = -3x + 1\).

  3. Looking at your graph. How are the two lines the same? How are they different? [plot of two lines with same intercept but different slopes]

4.2.1 – What Is Slope?

Look at the image below. In each of the four panels, a person is climbing stairs. How are the four images similar? How are they different?

You probably notice that the stairs in panels A and D are far less steep than the stairs in panels B and C. You may also notice that the stairs in panels A and C move up and to the right while the ones in panels B and D move down.

Linear functions are the same. Sometimes they go up as we move to the right, and sometimes they go down. Sometimes they are steep, and other times they are less so. Together, direction and steepness describe the slope.

The slope tells how much we rise (up or down) for every step we move to the right — it’s a rate of change. A larger slope means the line rises or falls more sharply — the vertical change happens faster for each horizontal step to the right.

On a graph, the rise is a change in \(y\) (abbreviated as \(\Delta y\)) and the run is a change in \(x\) (abbreviated as \(\Delta x\)).

The slope, often abbreviated as \(m\), is simply:

\[ m = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \]

Panel Direction Rise Compared to Run
A Positive (up) About \(\frac{2}{3}\) as fast
B Negative (down) About the same
C Positive About the same
D Negative About \(\frac{2}{3}\) as fast

4.2.2 – Finding Slope From a Graph

In the previous section we said that slope is rise over run (\(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\)).

We visually compared the stairs in each of the 4 panels, noting that sometimes the stairs went up (positive) and sometimes they went down (negative). We used comparisions of the rise to the run with statements like “about the same”.

Example 1:

Let’s look the same image again, but this time we will overlay a coordinate plane so that we can be more precise.

In panel A, the person climbs up 2 units for every 3 units they move right. The change in y (\(\Delta y\)) is 2 and the change in x (\(\Delta x\)) is 3. So the slope is \(m = \frac{2}{3}\).

In panel B, the person climbs down 3 units for every 3 units they move right. So the slope is \(m = \frac{-3}{3} = -1\).

In panel C, the person climbs up 3 units for every 3 units the move right. So the slope is \(m = \frac{3}{3} = 1\).

Finally, in panel D, the person climbs down 2 units for every 3 units they move right. So the slope is \(m = \frac{2}{3}\).

Example 2:

Find the slope of the line graphed below.

First, we pick two known points on the graph. Here we picked \((0, 2)\) and \((5, 6)\). Then we find the change in the x-values (\(\Delta x\)) and the change in the y values (\(\Delta y\)).

The slope of the line is:

\[ m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{4}{5} \]

4.2.3 – Types of Slope

Type Description Example
Positive Line rises left to right (m > 0)
Negative Line falls left to right (m < 0)
Zero Line is flat (horizontal) (y = 3)
Undefined Line is vertical (no run) (x = -2)
  • Positive slope: going uphill, savings increasing, temperature rising.
  • Negative slope: going downhill, spending money, cooling down.
  • Zero slope: no change — a flat road or steady balance.
  • Undefined slope: vertical line — you’d have to “divide by zero,” which doesn’t make sense.

Draw one example of each type of slope on graph paper. Label each line with its slope.

If two lines have different slopes, they’ll cross at one point.
That point has the same (x, y) values in both equations — it makes both true.

4.2.4 – Slope in the Real World

  • A wheelchair ramp that rises 1 foot for every 12 feet forward has a slope of ( ).
  • A hill that rises 10 feet for every 20 feet forward has a slope of ( = ). Which one is steeper?
  • A car traveling 60 miles per hour and a cyclist traveling 15 miles per hour both form straight lines on a distance-time graph.
  • The car’s line is steeper because its slope (rate of change) is greater.

The larger the slope, the faster the change. The smaller the slope, the slower or flatter the change.

Slope measures how y changes compared to x. A constant slope means the line rises or falls evenly — that’s what makes it linear.
