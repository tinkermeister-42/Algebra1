4.5 - Systems of Equations

So far, you’ve graphed one line at a time, each showing a single rule or relationship. But life isn’t always that simple.

Imagine comparing two phone plans. Each one has a different starting fee and a different rate per minute. You might wonder: When will they cost the same?

That kind of question can’t be answered with just one line, you have to see both relationships together. When two lines meet, they show a shared truth, a point that works for both situations.

That’s the idea behind a system of equations.

elimination, intersection, solution, substitution, system of equations

🔥 Warm Up

  1. Graph \(y = 2x + 1\) and \(y = -x + 4\) on the same axes.

  2. Is the point \((3, 4)\) a solution to \(y = 5x - 11\)?

  3. Is there a point that makes both \(y=2x+1\) and \(y=-x+4\) true? If so, how could you find it?

4.5.1 - Graphing Systems of Equations and Interpreting Their Solutions

A system of equations is two or more equations that share the same variables.

Each equation describes a rule or relationship, and the solution to the system is a point that satisfies all of those rules at the same time.

In the following examples, we will see some systems of equations and learn how to interpret their solutions.

Example 1: Finding the intersection

Here is a simple system of two equations. Let’s find the solution to the system by graphing.

\[ \begin{cases} y = 2x + 1 \\ y = -x + 4 \end{cases} \]

Interpretation:

The two lines cross at \((1, 3)\). This is the one point where both equations are true. We call this point the solution to the system.

We can check it using substitution.

\[ \begin{cases} \textcolor{blue}{3} = 2(\textcolor{red}{1})+1 = 3 ✔️ \\ \textcolor{blue}{3} = -(\textcolor{red}{1}) + 4 = 3 ✔️ \end{cases} \]

Example 2: Where do they cross?

Find the solution to the system of equations by graphing.

\[ \begin{cases} y = \tfrac{1}{2}x + 3 \\ y = -x + 6 \end{cases} \]

Interpretation:

The solution to the system is \((2, 4)\). This is the one point where both equations are true.

Example 3: Gym memberships

A local gym offers two membership plans:

  • Plan A: $10 sign-up fee + $5 per class
  • Plan B: $25 sign-up fee + $2 per class

Here is a system of equations that models these two plans. \[ \begin{cases} y = 5x + 10 \\ y = 2x + 25 \end{cases} \]

The lines meet at \((5, 35)\).

Interpretation:

After 5 classes, both plans cost $35. Before that, Plan A is cheaper; after that, Plan B is cheaper. The intersection shows exactly where the two plans balance.

Example 4: Plumbers and electricians

A plumber charges a flat fee of $40 plus $60 per hour. An electrician charges $100 plus $50 per hour.

Here is a system that models the service costs:

\[ \begin{cases} y = 60x + 40 \\ y = 50x + 100 \end{cases} \]

The lines intersect at \((6, 400)\).

Interpretation:

After 6 hours, both services cost $400. The \(x\)-value tells when they cost the same, and the \(y\)-value tells how much that shared cost is.

Two cell-phone plans are modeled by the equations below:

\[ \begin{cases} y = 15x + 40 \\ y = 25x + 10 \end{cases} \]

Here, \(y\) represents total cost (in dollars) and \(x\) represents the number of months on the plan.

Find the solution to the system by graphing and then interpret the solution.

The two lines cross at \((3, 85)\).

That means after 3 months, both plans cost $85. Before that, the second plan is cheaper; after that, the first plan is cheaper.

4.5.2 - Other Solving Methods (Optional)

Graphing is a great way to see where two lines meet, but sometimes the intersection doesn’t fall on clean grid points. Or maybe the lines are difficult to draw accurately. In those cases, we can solve the system using algebra instead of a graph.

There are two main algebraic methods:

  • Substitution - replace one variable with an equivalent expression
  • Elimination - add or subtract equations so one variable cancels out

Let’s look at each one.

Solving by Substitution

Substitution works because if two expressions are equal, you can replace one with the other. That turns a two-variable system into a single equation you already know how to solve.

Steps for Solving by Substitution

  1. Pick one of the equations and solve for one variable (like \(y\) or \(x\)).
  2. Substitute that expression into the other equation.
  3. Solve for the remaining variable.
  4. Plug back in to find the other variable.

Example 5: Substitute to solve

Solve the system of equations by substitution.

\[ \begin{cases} y = x + 2 \\ 2x + y = 8 \end{cases} \]

We already know \(y = x + 2\), so we can substitute that into the second equation:

\[ 2x + (x + 2) = 8 \]

Simplify and solve:

\[ \begin{aligned} 3x + 2 &= 8 \snminus{2} \\ 3x &= 6 \sndiv{3} \\ x &= 2 \end{aligned} \]

Now substitute back:

\[ y = 2 + 2 = 4 \]

Solution: \((2, 4)\)

Example 6: Substitute to solve

Solve the system of equations by substitution. \[ \begin{cases} y = 3x - 8 \\ x + y = -24 \end{cases} \]

Substitute \(y = 3x - 8\) into the second equation:

\[ x + (3x - 8) = -24 \]

Simplify and solve:

\[ \begin{aligned} 4x - 8 &= -24 \snplus{8} \\ 4x &= -16 \sndiv{4} \\ x &= -4 \end{aligned} \]

Then substitute and solve for y:

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= 3(-4) - 8 \\ y &= -20 \end{aligned} \]

Solution: \((-4, -20)\)

Solving by Elimination

Elimination works by stacking the two equations and adding or subtracting them so that one variable cancels out. This method is especially convenient when both equations already have similar \(x\) or \(y\) coefficients.

Steps for Solving by Elimination

  1. Line up both equations.
  2. Add or subtract the equations to eliminate that variable.
  3. Solve for the remaining variable.
  4. Substitute back to find the other variable.

Example 7: Eliminate it

Solve the system of equations by elimination. \[ \begin{cases} 2x + y = 10 \\ 2x - y = 2 \end{cases} \]

Add the equations to make \(y\) cancel out:

\[ \begin{aligned} (2x + y) + (2x - y) &= 10 + 2 \stepnote{Combine Like Terms} \\ 4x &= 12 \sndiv{4} \\ x &= 3 \end{aligned} \]

Now substitute \(x = 3\) into one of the equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} 2(3) + y &= 10 \snminus{6} \\ y &= 4 \end{aligned} \]

Solution: \((3, 4)\)

Example 8: Another example of elimination

Solve the system of equations by elimination.

\[ \begin{cases} x + y = 9 \\ x - y = 3 \end{cases} \]

Add the equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} (x + y) + (x - y) &= 9 + 3 \stepnote{Combine Like Terms} \\ 2x &= 12 \sndiv{2} \\ x &= 6 \end{aligned} \]

Now plug back in:

\[ \begin{aligned} 6 + y &= 9 \snminus{6} \\ y &= 3 \end{aligned} \]

Solution: \((6, 3)\)

In the previous two examples, one of the variables canceled out when combining the two equations. Sometimes you will have to multiply one or both equations by something for this to work.

Example 9: Multiplying first

Solve the system of equations by elimination. \[ \begin{cases} 3x + 2y = 16 \\ 2x + 3y = 14 \end{cases} \]

Neither variable will cancel right away, so we’ll make the \(x\)-coefficients match.

Multiply the first equation by 2 and the second by −3:

\[ \begin{cases} 6x + 4y = 32 \\ -6x - 9y = -42 \end{cases} \]

Now add the equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} (6x - 6x) + (4y - 9y) &= 32 - 42 \stepnote{Combine Like Terms} \\ -5y &= -10 \sndiv{-5} \\ y &= 2 \end{aligned} \]

Substitute \(y = 2\) into one of the original equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} 3x + 2(2) &= 16 \\ 3x + 4 &= 16 \snminus{4} \\ 3x &= 12 \sndiv{3} \\ x&= 4 \end{aligned} \]

Solution: \((4, 2)\)
