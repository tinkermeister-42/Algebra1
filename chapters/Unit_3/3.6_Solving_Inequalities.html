3.6 - Solving Inequalities

You have \(\$20\) in your pocket and want to buy some candy at the grocery store. You need to buy bread for \(\$3.50\), and each package of candy costs \(\$1.25\). How many packages of candy can you afford?

We might try to model this problem with the following equation.

Let \(c\) be the number of packages of candy you purchase.

\[ 3.5 + 1.25c = 20 \]

Solving for \(c\) gives us:

\[ \begin{aligned} 3.5 + 1.25c \textcolor{red}{-3.5} &= 20 \textcolor{red}{-3.5} \snminus{3.5} \\ 1.25c &= 16.5 \\ \\ 1.25c \textcolor{red}{\div 1.25} &= 16.5 \textcolor{red}{\div 1.25} \sndiv{1.25} \\ c &= 13.2 \end{aligned} \]

This tells us you can afford 13.2 packages of candy—but you can’t just buy part of a package.

Problems like this one show the limitations of equations. Equations are great when there’s a single exact answer, but real situations often have ranges of possible answers. To describe those ranges, we use inequalities.

Unlike an equation, inequalities help us solve problems with a range of answers.

3.6.1 - Inequality Basics

An inequality compares two expressions using one of these symbols:

Symbol Meaning Example
\(\neq\) Not equal \(x \neq y\)
\(>\) Greater than \(x > y\)
\(<\) Less than \(x < y\)
\(\ge\) Greater than or equal to \(x \ge y\)
\(\le\) Less than or equal to \(x \le y\)

In Unit 1, we plotted single solutions like \(x = 3\) on a number line:

That’s just one point. Inequalities, however, represent a whole range of values.

Example 2: Less Than

Example 3: Greater Than or Equal To

Same as Example 1, except the dot is filled in. That means 3 is included.

Example 4: Less Than or Equal To

Plot the solution to \(x \le 3\) on a number line.

The dot is filled again, showing 3 is part of the solution. The arrow points left for “less than”.

Test a value

3.6.2 - Solving Inequalities Using Addition and Subtraction

Solving inequalities works just like solving equations, you undo operations to get the variable by itself. The only difference is that the solution represents a range.

Example 5: A one-step inequality

Solve the inequality and plot its solution on a number line.

\[ x - 5 > 2 \]

We solve this like a one-step equation.

\[ \begin{aligned} x - 5 \textcolor{red}{+5} &> 2 \textcolor{red}{+5} \snplus{5} \\ x &> 7 \end{aligned} \]

Now we plot the solution.

The arrow covers all numbers larger than 7. The dot is left open because 7 itself is not included.

Example 6: Another one-step inequality

Solve the inequality and plot its solution.

\[ x + 2 \le 5 \]

First we solve.

\[ \begin{aligned} x + 2 \textcolor{red}{-2} &\le 5 \textcolor{red}{-2} \snminus{2} \\ x &\le 3 \end{aligned} \]

Now plot it.

3.6.3 – Solving Inequalities with Multiplication and Division

If you multiply or divide by a positive number, then you solve just like an equation.

Example 7: A two-step inequality

Solve the inequality and plot the solution.

\[ 3x - 4 > 5 \]

Solve first:

\[ \begin{aligned} 3x - 4 \textcolor{red}{+4} &> 5 \textcolor{red}{+4} \snplus{4} \\ 3x &> 9 \\ \\ 3x \textcolor{red}{\div 3} &> 9 \textcolor{red}{\div 3} \sndiv{3} \\ x &> 3 \end{aligned} \]

Now plot it:

The one big difference between solving equations and inequalities comes when you multiply or divide by a negative number. In that case, you have to flip the sign.

Example 8: Flipping the sign

Solve the inequality and plot the solution.

\[ 5 - 2x < -3 \]

Solve:

\[ \begin{aligned} 5 - 2x \textcolor{red}{-5} &< -3 \textcolor{red}{-5} \snminus{5} \\ -2x &< -8 \\ \\ -2x \textcolor{red}{\div -2} &< -8 \textcolor{red}{\div -2} \sndiv{-2} \\ x &> 4 \stepnote{Flip the sign} \end{aligned} \]

Plot the solution:

Don’t forget to flip the sign when you multiply or divide by a negative number. If you are in doubt, you can always test your answer to see if it works.

In the previous example we found \(x > 4\). Let’s test our answer with \(x = 5\).

\[ \begin{aligned} 5 - 2(\textcolor{red}{5}) &< -3 \stepnote{substitute 5} \\ 5 - 10 &< -3 \\ -5 &< -3 \stepnote{$\leftarrow$ a true statement} \end{aligned} \]

If we hadn’t flipped the sign, we’d get \(-5 > -3\) (false).

3.6.4 – Real-World Problems With Inequalities

Let’s revisit the candy problem:

You have \(\$20\), break costs \(\$3.50\), and candy is \(\$1.25\) per package. How many packages can you afford?

Let \(c\) be the number of packages you purchase. The expression \(3.5 + 1.25c\) will give you the cost of purchasing the candy with a loaf of bread.

To find the largest number of packages you can purchase, you have to set up the inequality so that the total spent is less than or equal to \(\$20\).

\[ 3.5 + 1.25c \le 20 \]

Let’s solve it.

\[ \begin{aligned} 3.5 + 1.25c \textcolor{red}{-3.5} &\le 20 \textcolor{red}{-3.5} \snminus{3.5} \\ 1.25c &\le 16.5 \\ \\ 1.25c \textcolor{red}{\div 1.25} &\le 16.5 \textcolor{red}{\div 1.25} \sndiv{1.25} \\ c &\le 13.2 \end{aligned} \]

Since you can only purchase whole packages of candy, the closest whole number of packages that is less than or equal to 13.2 is 13 packages.

  8. A group rents a bus for \(\$200\) plus \(\$15\) per person. If they can spend at most \(\$500\), how many people can go?
