3.7 - Solving Literal Equations

Imagine you’re asked to solve for time \(t\) in the equation \(d = rt\). You might recognize this as the distance formula: distance = rate × time.

If you know any two of these values, you can find the third, but what if you need to rearrange the formula itself? That’s what we call a literal equation.

Literal equations use letters (or “literals”) to represent variables, so solving them means isolating one variable in terms of the others.

equation, isolate, literal equation, variable

  1. You know that \(A = lw\) gives the area of a rectangle.

    • \(d = rt\)

  3. Think about how you get \(x\) by itself in \(3x + 5 = 20\). What would you do first? How could you apply that same idea in \(3x + a = b\)?

3.7.1 - What Is a Literal Equation?

A literal equation is an equation made mostly of letters. You can think of it as a formula, like \(A = lw\) for the area of a rectangle or \(P = 2l + 2w\) for its perimeter.

When we solve a literal equation, our goal is to isolate one variable using the same inverse operations we use in regular equations.

Example 1: Finding the width

A rectangle has area \(A = lw\). Solve for the width \(w\).

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= lw \\ \\ A \textcolor{red}{\div l} &= lw \textcolor{red}{\div l} \sndiv{l} \\ \frac{A}{l} &= w \end{aligned} \]

So \(w = \dfrac{A}{l}\).

Example 2: Finding the time

Distance is equal to the rate times the time \(d = rt\). Solve for the time \(t\).

\[ \begin{aligned} d &= rt \\ \\ d \textcolor{red}{\div r} &= rt \textcolor{red}{\div r} \sndiv{r} \\ \frac{d}{r} &= t \end{aligned} \]

So \(t = \dfrac{d}{r}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= IR \\ \\ V \textcolor{red}{\div R} &= IR \textcolor{red}{\div R} \sndiv{R} \\ \tfrac{V}{R} &= I \end{aligned} \]

So \(I = \tfrac{V}{R}\).

3.7.2 - Literal Equations with Addition or Subtraction

Sometimes a variable appears in a longer expression. We can move terms using addition or subtraction before dividing or multiplying.

Example 3: Finding the width

A rectangle has perimeter \(P = 2l + 2w\). Solve for the width \(w\).

\[ \begin{aligned} P &= 2l + 2w \\ \\ P \textcolor{red}{-2l} &= 2l + 2w \textcolor{red}{-2l} \snminus{2l} \\ P - 2l &= 2w \\ \\ (P - 2l) \textcolor{red}{\div 2} &= 2w \textcolor{red}{\div 2} \sndiv{2} \\ \frac{P - 2l}{2} &= w \end{aligned} \]

So \(w = \dfrac{P - 2l}{2}\).

Example 4: A linear equation

A line has the equation \(y = mx + b\). Solve for \(x\).

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b \\ \\ y \textcolor{red}{-b} &= mx + b \textcolor{red}{-b} \snminus{b} \\ y - b &= mx \\ \\ (y - b) \textcolor{red}{\div m} &= mx \textcolor{red}{\div m} \sndiv{m} \\ \frac{y - b}{m} &= x \end{aligned} \]

So \(x = \dfrac{y - b}{m}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} 3a + b &= 2c \\ \\ 3a + b \textcolor{red}{-3a} &= 2c \textcolor{red}{-3a} \snminus{3a} \\ b &= 2c - 3a \end{aligned} \]

So \(b = 2c - 3a\).

3.7.3 - Multi-Step Literal Equations

Now let’s look at equations that take more than one operation to isolate the variable.

Example 5: A two step equation

Solve \(V = Bh + 12\) for \(h\).

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= Bh + 12 \\ \\ V \textcolor{red}{-12} &= Bh + 12 \textcolor{red}{-12} \snminus{12} \\ V - 12 &= Bh \\ \\ (V - 12) \textcolor{red}{\div B} &= Bh \textcolor{red}{\div B} \sndiv{B} \\ \frac{V - 12}{B} &= h \end{aligned} \]

Example 6: A fractional coefficient

Solve \(A = \frac{1}{2}bh + 3\) for \(b\).

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= \frac{1}{2}bh + 3 \\ \\ A \textcolor{red}{-3} &= \frac{1}{2}bh + 3 \textcolor{red}{-3} \snminus{3} \\ A - 3 &= \frac{1}{2}bh \\ \\ (A - 3) \textcolor{red}{\times 2} &= \frac{1}{2}bh \textcolor{red}{\times 2} \sntimes{2} \\ 2(A - 3) &= bh \\ \\ 2(A - 3) \textcolor{red}{\div h} &= bh \textcolor{red}{\div h} \sndiv{h} \\ \frac{2(A - 3)}{h} &= b \end{aligned} \]

Example 7: Converting Celsius to Fahrenheit.

The formula for convering form Celsius to Fahrenheit is \(C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32)\). Solve for the temperature in Fahrenheit \(F\).

\[ \begin{aligned} C &= \frac{5}{9}(F - 32) \\ \\ C \textcolor{red}{\times \frac{9}{5}} &= \frac{5}{9}(F - 32) \textcolor{red}{\times \frac{9}{5}} \sntimes{\frac{9}{5}} \\ \frac{9}{5}C &= F - 32 \\ \\ \frac{9}{5}C \textcolor{red}{+ 32} &= F - 32 \textcolor{red}{+ 32} \snplus{32} \\ F &= \frac{9}{5}C + 32 \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} PV &= nR(T + 2) \\ \\ PV \textcolor{red}{\div nR} &= nR(T + 2) \textcolor{red}{\div nR} \sndiv{nR} \\ \tfrac{PV}{nR} &= T+2 \\ \\ \tfrac{PV}{nR} \textcolor{red}{-2} &= T + 2 \textcolor{red}{-2} \snminus{2} \\ \tfrac{PV}{nR} - 2 &= T \end{aligned} \]

So \(T = \tfrac{PV}{nR} - 2\).
