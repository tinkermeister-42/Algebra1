2.6 - Equivalent Expressions

When you visit another country, you might hear the same word said in different ways. Hello, hola, bonjour, all mean the same thing, but they look and sound different.

Math is like that too. An idea can be written many different ways. \(2(x+3)\) and \(2x+6\) look different, but they both “say” the same thing. We call such pairs equivalent expressions.

In this lesson, you’ll learn how to recognize when expressions are equivalent by simplifying, using substitution, and thinking logically. This idea is a big deal in Algebra, especially when we start solving equations in Unit 3.

2.6.1 - What Does “Equivalent” Mean?

Two expressions are equivalent if they have the same value for every input (for all allowed values of the variable). They do not have to look the same.

Example 1: Same value, different look

Compare

\[ A(x)=3(x+4)\quad\text{and}\quad B(x)=3x+12. \]

Try two easy inputs (we’ll use \(x=0\) and \(x=5\)):

\(x\) \(A(x)=3(x+4)\) \(B(x)=3x+12\)
0 \(3(0+4)=3\cdot4=12\) \(3\cdot0+12=12\)
5 \(3(5+4)=3\cdot9=27\) \(3\cdot5+12=27\)

The outputs match. This is evidence that they are equivalent.

Testing a few values gives evidence, not a full proof. A proof shows it’s true for all inputs.

Example 2: Not equivalent (by substitution)

Compare

\[ C(x)=4x+1 \quad\text{and}\quad D(x)=4(x+1). \]

Try \(x=2\):

\[ C(2)=4(\textcolor{red}{2})+1=8+1=9 \qquad D(2)=4(\textcolor{red}{2}+1)=4\cdot3=12. \]

Since we got different outputs for the same input, the expressions are not equivalent.

When we test a value and the results are different, we call this a counterexample.

Example 3: Matching sometimes isn’t enough

Sometimes two expressions match at some inputs but not at others.

\[ P(x)=x \quad\text{and}\quad Q(x)=x^2. \]

  • \(x=0:\; P(0)=0,\; Q(0)=0\) (match)
  • \(x=1:\; P(1)=1,\; Q(1)=1\) (match)
  • \(x=2:\; P(2)=2,\; Q(2)=4\) (no match)

So “matching a few times” is only evidence, not a guarantee.

Pick two easy inputs (\(0\), \(1\), \(2\), or \(-1\)) for each pair.

  1. \(x+x+x\) and \(3x\)
  2. \(5(x-2)\) and \(5x-10\)
  3. \(2x+3\) and \(2(x+3)\)

Suppose we test \(x=1\) and \(x=2\):

  1. \(\textcolor{red}{1} + \textcolor{red}{1} + \textcolor{red}{1} = 3\) and \(3(\textcolor{red}{1}) = 3\) \(\implies\) evidence
    \(\textcolor{red}{2} + \textcolor{red}{2} + \textcolor{red}{2} = 6\) and \(3(\textcolor{red}{2}) = 6\) \(\implies\) evidence
    The expressions appear to be equivalent.

  2. \(5(\textcolor{red}{1} - 2) = -5\) and \(5(\textcolor{red}{1}) - 10 = -5\) \(\implies\) evidence
    \(5(\textcolor{red}{2} - 2) = 0\) and \(5(\textcolor{red}{2}) - 10 = 0\) \(\implies\) evidence
    The expressions appear to be equivalent.

  3. \(2(\textcolor{red}{1}) + 3 = 5\) and \(2(\textcolor{red}{1} + 3) = 8\) \(\implies\) counterexample
    \(2(\textcolor{red}{2}) + 3 = 7\) and \(2(\textcolor{red}{2} + 3) = 10\) \(\implies\) counterexample
    The expressions are not equivalent.

Yes. You can never prove that two expressions are equivalent by testing values, but one counterexample is enough to prove they are not equivalent.

2.6.2 - Proving Equivalence

We’ve talked a lot about testing whether expressions are equivalent. But how do we prove they are equivalent?

Two expressions are equivalent if they give the same result for every value of their variables. But we can’t test every value. To prove equivalence, use algebra to rewrite each expression until they match.

Example 4: (Distribute)

Prove \(2(3 - y)\) and \(6 - 2y\) are equivalent.

\[ 2(3 - y) = 6 - 2y \]

They match.

Example 5: (Combine Like Terms)

Combine like terms to prove \(2x - 3y + 7\) and \(2 - 3x + 5 - 3y + 5x\) are equivalent.

\[ 2 - 3x + 5 - 3y + 5x = 2x - 3y + 7 \]

They match.

Example 6: (Simplify both sides)

Prove \(2(x - 1) + 3x\) and \(2x + 3(x - 2) + 4\) are equivalent.

\[ \begin{aligned} 2(x - 1) + 3x &= 2x - 2 + 3x \\ &= 5x - 2 \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} 2x + 3(x - 2) + 4 &= 2x + 3x - 6 + 4 \\ &= 5x - 2 \end{aligned} \]

They match.

Example 7: (Factor)

Prove \(6z - 2\) and \(2(3z - 1)\) are equivalent.

The GCF for \(6z\) and \(-2\) is \(2\).

\[ 6z - 2 = 2(3z - 1) \]

The expressions are equivalent.

We can also prove expressions are not equivalent using algebra.

Example 8: Disproving equivalence

Prove \(2x + 3\) and \(2(x + 3)\) are not equivalent.

\[ 2(x + 3) = 2x + 6 \neq 2x + 3 \]

The do not match. The expressions are not equivalent.

Simplify both expressions.

\[ \begin{aligned} 3 - 2(x + 1) &= 3 - 2x - 2 \\ &= 1 - 2x \end{aligned} \]

\[ x + 1 - 3x = 1 - 2x \]

They match, so the expressions are equivalent.
