6.7 - Applications of Quadratics

So far, we have focused on how to work with quadratic equations. We have learned how to factor them, complete the square, use square roots, and apply the quadratic formula.

But quadratic equations were not invented just to be solved.

They model real situations involving height, distance, area, revenue, and time. In this lesson, we will use quadratics to make sense of real world problems and interpret what the solutions actually mean.

6.7.1 - Quadratics as Models

A quadratic model is a function of the form

\[ f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c \]

that represents a real situation.

Because parabolas curve, quadratic models are useful when something rises and then falls, increases and then decreases, or has a maximum or minimum value.

The vertex plays a key role. It often represents the highest point, the lowest point, the maximum profit, or the minimum cost.

The intercepts are important as well. In projectile motion, for example, the \(y\)-intercept is often the starting height, and the \(x\)-intercepts indicate when the projectile hits the ground.

Using Features of a Parabola in Real Situations

When a quadratic is used as a model, different parts of the graph answer different questions. The table below shows what each feature often represents in real life.

Feature of the parabola What it tells us Example situations
Vertex The highest or lowest value of the situation Maximum height of a ball, highest profit, minimum cost
\(y\)-intercept The starting value when \(x = 0\) Starting height, initial amount of money, starting population
\(x\)-intercepts When the quantity becomes zero When an object hits the ground, when profit is zero, break-even points
Axis of symmetry The point where the situation switches direction When something stops increasing and starts decreasing
Opening direction Whether the situation has a maximum or minimum Downward opening for max height, upward opening for min cost

For each of the following, decide which feature of a parabola would be most useful.

  1. A ball is thrown from a cliff. How tall is the cliff?

  2. A skateboarder rides on a ramp. How long does it take to reach the bottom?

  3. A small business makes money selling shirts. How many shirts do they need to sell to break even?

  4. A rocket is fired into the air. How high does the rocket go before coming back down to earth?

  1. The \(y\)-intercept

  2. The \(x\) coordinate for the vertex

  3. The \(x\)-intercepts

  4. The \(y\)-coordinate of the vertex

6.7.2 - Projectile Motion

One common application of quadratics is projectile motion.

An object moving under gravity can often be modeled by an equation like

\[ h(t) = at^2 + bt + c \]

where \(t\) is time in seconds and \(h(t)\) is height in feet.

Example 1

A ball is thrown upward from a height of \(5\) feet. Its height after \(t\) seconds is given by

\[ h(t) = -16t^2 + 20t + 5 \]

  1. When does the ball hit the ground?
  2. What is the maximum height of the ball?

Step 1 : Find when it hits the ground

The ball hits the ground when its height is zero. If we set \(h(t) = 0\), we can solve for \(t\) to find the time the ball hits the ground.

\[ -16t^2 + 20t + 5 = 0 \]

Use the quadratic formula.

\[ t = \frac{-20 \pm \sqrt{20^2 - 4(-16)(5)}}{2(-16)} \]

\[ t = \frac{-20 \pm \sqrt{720}}{-32} \]

\[ t = \frac{-20 \pm 12\sqrt{5}}{-32} \]

There are two solutions, one positive and one negative. In this situation, a negative time would mean the ball hit the ground in the past. That doesn’t make sense so we ignore that solution.

The postive time:

\[ t = \frac{-20 - 12\sqrt{5}}{-32} = \frac{5 + 3\sqrt{5}}{8} \approx 1.46 \]

So the ball hits the ground after about \(1.46\) seconds.

Quadratic equations often produce two solutions. In applications, you must decide which solutions make sense in context. Negative time, negative length, or negative area usually do not apply.

Step 2 : Find the maximum height

The maximum happens at the vertex time:

\[ t = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{20}{2(-16)} = \frac{5}{8} = 0.625 \]

Now find the height:

\[ h\left(\frac{5}{8}\right) = -16\left(\frac{25}{64}\right) + 20\left(\frac{5}{8}\right) + 5 \]

\[ h\left(\frac{5}{8}\right) = -\frac{25}{4} + \frac{25}{2} + 5 \]

\[ h\left(\frac{5}{8}\right) = \frac{45}{4} = 11.25 \]

Maximum height is \(11.25\) feet.

A ball is thrown from a height of \(6\) feet with equation \(h(t) = -16t^2 + 24t + 6\). When does it hit the ground?

Set \(h(t)=0\):

\[ -16t^2 + 24t + 6 = 0 \]

Divide by \(-2\):

\[ 8t^2 - 12t - 3 = 0 \]

Quadratic formula:

\[ t = \frac{12 \pm \sqrt{(-12)^2 - 4(8)(-3)}}{2(8)} \]

\[ t = \frac{12 \pm \sqrt{144 + 96}}{16} \]

\[ t = \frac{12 \pm \sqrt{240}}{16} = \frac{12 \pm 4\sqrt{15}}{16} \]

Positive solution:

\[ t = \frac{12 + 4\sqrt{15}}{16} = \frac{3 + \sqrt{15}}{4} \approx 1.72 \]

6.7.3 - Area Problems

Quadratics also appear in area situations.

Example 2

A rectangular garden has a length that is \(4\) feet longer than its width. The area is \(96\) square feet. Find the dimensions.

Let the width be \(x\).
Then the length is \(x + 4\).

\[ x(x + 4) = 96 \]

\[ x^2 + 4x - 96 = 0 \]

Factor:

\[ (x + 12)(x - 8) = 0 \]

So:

\[ x = -12 \quad \text{or} \quad x = 8 \]

Only \(x=8\) makes sense because a length cannot be negative.

Width is \(8\) feet and length is \(12\) feet.

A rectangular pool is surrounded by a 2-foot-wide walkway on all sides.
The total area of the pool and the walkway is 192 square feet.
The pool itself is 4 feet longer than it is wide.

Find the dimensions of the pool.

Let the pool’s width be \(w\) feet.
Then the pool’s length is \(w + 4\) feet.

The walkway is \(2\) feet wide on all sides, so it adds \(4\) feet to each dimension.

Outside width: \(w + 4\)
Outside length: \((w + 4) + 4 = w + 8\)

The total area of the pool and walkway is \(192\) square feet, so:

\[ (w + 4)(w + 8) = 192 \]

Expand:

\[ w^2 + 12w + 32 = 192 \]

Subtract \(192\) from both sides:

\[ w^2 + 12w - 160 = 0 \]

Factor:

\[ (w + 20)(w - 8) = 0 \]

So \(w = -20\) or \(w = 8\).
Width can’t be negative so we ignore \(-20\).

The pool’s width is \(8\) feet.
The pool’s length is \(8 + 4 = 12\) feet.

6.7.4 - Revenue and Profit

Quadratics are also used to model revenue and profit.

Example 3

A school sells tickets to a play. When tickets cost \(\$10\) each, \(80\) tickets are sold. For every \(\$1\) increase in price, \(5\) fewer tickets are sold.

Let \(x\) be the number of \(\$1\) increases.

Price: \(10 + x\)
Tickets sold: \(80 - 5x\)

Revenue (in dollars):

\[ R(x) = (10 + x)(80 - 5x) \]

\[ R(x) = -5x^2 + 30x + 800 \]

Maximum revenue occurs at the vertex:

\[ x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{30}{2(-5)} = 3 \]

So ticket price is:

\[ 10 + 3 = 13 \]

Tickets sold:

\[ 80 - 5(3) = 65 \]

Maximum revenue:

\[ R(3) = (13)(65) = 845 \]

So the maximum revenue is \(\$845\).

A food truck sells lunch specials. When the price is \(\$8\), they sell \(60\) meals per day. For every \(\$1\) increase in price, they sell \(4\) fewer meals.

Let \(x\) be the number of \(\$1\) price increases.

Write the revenue equation and find the price that maximizes daily revenue.

Price: \(8 + x\)
Meals sold: \(60 - 4x\)

\[ R(x) = (8 + x)(60 - 4x) \]

\[ R(x) = -4x^2 + 28x + 480 \]

Vertex:

\[ x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{28}{2(-4)} = 3.5 \]

Since \(x\) must be a whole dollar amount, check \(x = 3\) and \(x = 4\):

\[ R(3) = (11)(48) = 528 \]

\[ R(4) = (12)(44) = 528 \]

Both give the same maximum revenue of \(\$528\). The optimal price is \(\$11\) or \(\$12\) per meal.

✍️ Practice On Your Own

Level 1 : Understanding the Model

  1. Which part of a quadratic represents the maximum or minimum value?
  2. What does a solution of \(h(t) = 0\) usually represent in a height problem?
  3. Why do some quadratic solutions get rejected in real problems?

Level 2 : Applications

  1. A ball is thrown upward from the ground with equation
    \[ h(t) = -16t^2 + 32t \]
    When does it hit the ground?

  2. A rectangle has area \(40\) square feet. Its length is \(3\) feet longer than its width. Find the dimensions.

  3. The height of a firework is given by
    \[ h(t) = -16t^2 + 48t + 10 \]
    What is the maximum height?

  4. The profit from selling \(x\) items is
    \[ P(x) = -x^2 + 20x - 60 \]
    How many items should be sold to maximize profit?

Level 3 : Challenging

  1. A ball is thrown upward from a cliff \(40\) feet high. Its height is modeled by
    \[ h(t) = -16t^2 + 16t + 40 \]
    When does it hit the ground?

  2. A rectangle has a perimeter of \(40\) units. If the width is \(x\), show that the length must be \(20 - x\). Then find the value of \(x\) that maximizes the area.

  3. A quadratic model has zeros at \(t = 1\) and \(t = 5\). What might those values represent in a real situation?

Challenge Problems

  1. Write a real world situation that could be modeled by
    \[ y = -2x^2 + 12x - 5 \]
    Explain what the vertex represents.

  2. A projectile reaches a maximum height of \(64\) feet after \(2\) seconds and lands on the ground at \(t = 5\). Write a possible quadratic model.

Level 1

  1. The vertex, because it is the highest or lowest point of the parabola.
  2. The time when the object hits the ground.
  3. Because only values that make sense in context are allowed, such as positive time and positive length.

Level 2

  1. Set \(h(t)=0\): \[ -16t^2 + 32t = 0 \] \[ -16t(t-2)=0 \] \[ t=0 \text{ or } t=2 \] The ball hits the ground at \(t=2\) seconds.

  2. Let width be \(x\), length is \(x+3\): \[ x(x+3)=40 \] \[ x^2+3x-40=0 \] \[ (x+8)(x-5)=0 \] Width is \(5\) ft and length is \(8\) ft.

  3. Vertex time: \[ t=-\frac{b}{2a}=-\frac{48}{2(-16)}=\frac{3}{2} \] Height: \[ h\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)=-16\left(\frac{9}{4}\right)+48\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)+10 \] \[ h\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)=-36+72+10=46 \] Maximum height is \(46\) feet.

  4. Vertex: \[ x=-\frac{b}{2a}=-\frac{20}{2(-1)}=10 \] So profit is maximized at \(10\) items. \[ P(10)=-100+200-60=40 \] The maximum profit is \(40\) dollars.

Level 3

  1. Set \(h(t)=0\): \[ -16t^2+16t+40=0 \] Divide by \(-8\): \[ 2t^2-2t-5=0 \] Quadratic formula: \[ t=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{(-2)^2-4(2)(-5)}}{4} \] \[ t=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{44}}{4}=\frac{2\pm 2\sqrt{11}}{4}=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{11}}{2} \] Positive time: \[ t=\frac{1+\sqrt{11}}{2}\approx 2.16 \]

  2. Perimeter gives \(2x + 2l = 40\), so \(l = 20 - x\).

    Area: \[ A(x) = x(20 - x) = -x^2 + 20x \]

    Vertex: \[ x = -\frac{20}{2(-1)} = 10 \]

    Maximum area: \[ A(10) = 10(10) = 100 \]

    Maximum area is \(100\) square units when the rectangle is \(10 \times 10\).

  3. They could represent two times when a projectile is at ground height, or two times when a quantity equals zero, such as profit breaking even.
