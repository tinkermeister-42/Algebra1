6.2 – Graphing Quadratics

In the last lesson, you looked at graphs of quadratic functions and learned the key features of a parabola: the vertex, the axis of symmetry, and how the graph can have a maximum or a minimum.

Earlier in the course, you graphed lines by starting with a table of values and later moved to using slope and intercept directly from the equation.

Now we ask a similar question for quadratics:

How can we use a quadratic equation to graph its parabola?

axis of symmetry, decreasing, increasing, parabola, standard form, vertex, vertex form

🔥 Warm-Up

  1. Find the slope and y-intercept for the line \(y = 3x - 2\). How can you use those to graph the line?

  2. If a line has slope \(m = \tfrac{1}{2}\) and passes through the point \((4, 1)\), what is its y-intercept?

  3. Find the vertex of \(y = 2x^2 + 4x - 1\) if its axis of symmetry is \(x=-1\).

6.2.1 – Graphing With a Table of Values

You first graphed linear equations by making a table of values, plotting the points, and drawing the line through them. This was easy because you only need two points to make a straight line.

A quadratic equation is different. A parabola curves, turns, and changes direction. You need several points to see its shape.

Example 1: Using a table of values

Let’s graph:

\[ f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 1 \]

First, we make a table by choosing x-values and calculating their matching y-values.

x f(x)
0 1
1 -2
2 -3
3 -2
4 1

Next, we plot the points:

Then we connect them:

The graph dips down, reaches its minimum at the vertex, and rises again. This curved shape cannot be seen from only two points.

This method works, but it has some clear drawbacks:

  • You need many points to see the parabola’s shape.
  • If you choose the “wrong” x-values, you might miss the vertex.
  • Squaring numbers and handling signs can lead to mistakes.
  • It takes much more time than graphing a line.

Just like with linear equations, we want a quicker, more reliable way to graph.

For quadratics, we can graph much faster by using a few key features of the equation.

6.2.2 – Graphing Using Key Features

A parabola becomes much easier to sketch when you know three important features:

  • the vertex
  • the axis of symmetry
  • whether it opens upward or downward

With these features, you can graph a parabola using only two additional points.

Example 2: Graphing from Features

From Example 1, the parabola we graphed had:

  • Vertex at \((2, -3)\)
  • Axis of symmetry: \(x = 2\)
  • Opening direction: upward

Plot the vertex and draw the axis of symmetry:

Next, choose a point on one side of the vertex, such as \(x = 3\):

\[ f(3) = 3^2 - 4(3) + 1 = -2 \]

This gives the point \((3, -2)\).

Because the graph is symmetric, we also plot its mirror image at \((1, -2)\).

Connect the points smoothly to complete the parabola:

When graphing by hand, you might add a couple more points to improve accuracy. If you need very precise graphs, calculators or computer programs are usually better.

A graph can tell us a lot about how a function behaves.

Before the vertex: the graph moves downward as x increases. We say the function is decreasing on the interval \((-\infty, 2)\).

After the vertex: the graph moves upward. We say the function is increasing on the interval \((2, \infty)\).

Because the graph crosses the x-axis twice, the function is:

  • negative between the intercepts
  • positive outside of them

These ideas help us describe the function’s behavior just by looking at the graph.

Graph the quadratic

\[ f(x) = 3x^2 - 6x + 7 \]

with features:

  • Vertex \((1, 4)\)
  • Axis of symmetry \(x = 1\)
  • Opening direction: upward

6.2.3 – Graphing From Vertex Form

As we saw in earlier units, a function can be written in different forms. One useful form for quadratics is vertex form:

\[ f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k \]

Vertex form makes the key features visible right away:

  • Vertex: \((h, k)\)
  • Axis of symmetry: \(x = h\)
  • Opening direction: upward if \(a > 0\), downward if \(a < 0\)

Because the important information is built into the structure of the equation, graphing becomes much faster.

Example 3: Graphing a parabola that opens upward

Graph
\[ f(x) = 2(x - 3)^2 - 4 \]

Key features:

  • Vertex: \((3, -4)\)
  • Axis of symmetry: \(x = 3\)
  • Opening direction: upward

Pick \(x = 4\):

\[ f(4) = 2(1)^2 - 4 = -2 \Rightarrow (4, -2) \]

Reflect the point over \(x = 3\) to get \((2, -2)\).

The function decreases until \(x = 3\) and increases afterward.
It is negative between the x-intercepts and positive elsewhere.

Example 4: Graphing a parabola that opens downward

Graph
\[ f(x) = -\tfrac{1}{3}(x + 2)^2 - 1 \]

  • Vertex: \((-2, -1)\)
  • Axis of symmetry: \(x = -2\)
  • Opening direction: downward

Pick \(x = 1\):

\[ f(1) = -\dfrac{1}{3}(1 + 2)^2 - 1 = -4 \]

This gives the point \((1, -4)\) and its reflection \((-5, -4)\).

Students often forget that \(h\) is subtracted and \(k\) is added in the formula. For example:

\[ f(x) = 2(x + 3)^2 - 2 \]

The vertex is not \((3, -2)\). It is \((-3, -2)\).

Always switch the sign on the \(h\) value and keep the sign for the \(k\) value.

  • Vertex: \((-1, 3)\)
  • Axis of symmetry: \(x = -1\)
  • Opening direction: upward

Picking \(x = -3\) gives \((-3, 7)\), and its reflection is \((1, 7)\).

Vertex form is ideal for graphing, but many quadratics appear in standard form instead. The vertex isn’t visible at first glance.

The good news is that standard form has a shortcut for finding the axis of symmetry,
and once you have that, the vertex follows immediately.

This means you can graph any quadratic, no matter how it’s written.

6.2.4 – Finding the Axis of Symmetry (Standard Form)

A quadratic in standard form is written:

\[ f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c \]

There is always a vertical line running down the center of the parabola.
In standard form, it can be found using:

\[ x = -\frac{b}{2a} \]

This value gives the x-coordinate of the vertex.

Example 5: Axis of symmetry from standard form

Find the axis of symmetry for
\[ f(x) = -3x^2 + 12x + 5. \]

  • \(a = -3\)
  • \(b = 12\)

\[ x = -\frac{12}{2(-3)} = 2 \]

Axis of symmetry: \(x = 2\)

\(a=2\), \(b=4\)

\[ x = -\frac{4}{2(2)} = -1 \]

Axis of symmetry: \(x = -1\)

6.2.5 – Finding the Vertex (Standard Form)

Once we know the axis of symmetry \(x = h\), finding the vertex is easy: it always has the form \((h, f(h))\).

Example 6: Vertex from standard form

Find the vertex of
\[ f(x) = x^2 - 6x + 10. \]

Axis of symmetry:

\[ x = -\frac{-6}{2(1)} = 3 \]

Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(3) &= 3^2 - 6(3) + 10 \\ &= 9 - 18 + 10 \\ &= 1 \end{aligned} \]

Vertex: \((3, 1)\)

Axis:
\[x = \frac{-8}{2(2)} = -2\]

Then
\[f(-2) = -13\]

Vertex: \((-2, -13)\)
