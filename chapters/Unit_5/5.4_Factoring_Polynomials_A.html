5.4 - Factoring: GCF & Basic Trinomials

You’ve spent a lot of time learning how to simplify expressions: distribute, combine like terms, and rewrite things in cleaner forms. But sometimes we want to go in the opposite direction.

Take something like:

\[ 12x + 18. \]

It looks simple, but there’s structure hidden inside it. Could this expression be written as a product of smaller pieces?

Now look at a trinomial such as

\[ x^2 + 11x + 24. \]

Where did it come from? Could it be the result of multiplying two binomials? If so, how could we work backward to uncover them?

In this lesson, we will find the answers to these questions by learning the basics of factoring polynomials.

coefficient, factor, greatest common factor, polynomial, term, trinomial

🔥 Warm-Up

  1. Find the GCF of each pair:
    1. 12 and 18
    2. 8x and 20
    3. 21x² and 14x

  2. Rewrite in standard form:
    1. \(5 + 3x + x^2\)
    2. \(-2x + 7 + 4x^2\)

  3. What two numbers multiply to \(14\) and add to \(9\)?

5.4.1 - Factoring Out a GCF

In Lesson 1.3, you learned how to find a greatest common factor (GCF). The GCF is the largest number or expression that divides every term.

When working with polynomials, writing an expression as a product is often the first step toward a cleaner or more useful form. Before jumping in, let’s quickly remind ourselves what a GCF looks like.

A fast way to review the idea is to look at the factors of each term and see what they share:

  • \(12x\) has factors \(2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot x\)
  • \(18\) has factors \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3\)
  • The part they have in common is \(6\)

This is the same idea you used in Unit 1 with factor trees.

Example 1: An integer GCF

Here is a factor-tree style diagram for the expression \(12x + 18\):

This picture shows the kind of result we want: the expression written as two factors that multiply to the whole expression.

To do that, we take out the greatest common factor:

\[ \begin{align*} \text{GCF}(12x,18) &= 6 \\ 12x + 18 &= 6(2x + 3) \end{align*} \]

Just like a factor tree, we have separated the common part from what remains.

Example 2: A GCF with a variable

Factor \(12x^3 - 3x^2\).

This time, each term has both a numerical factor and a variable factor in common.

Break each term into its pieces:

  • \(12x^3 = 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x\)
  • \(3x^2 = 3 \cdot x \cdot x\)

The shared part is \(3x\), so that becomes the GCF:

\[ \begin{align*} 12x^3 - 3x^2 &= 3x(4x^2 - 1) \end{align*} \]

Pulling out the GCF keeps the structure organized and prepares the expression for more factoring later.

\[ 15y^3 - 5y = 5y(3y^2 - 1) \]

Example 3: A negative leading term

Factor \(-x^2 - 4x - 3\).

When the first term is negative, it is usually easiest to take out \(-1\) first. This keeps the inside positive and easier to work with:

\[ -x^2 - 4x - 3 = -1(x^2 + 4x + 3) \]

Now the polynomial is written as a product, and the trinomial inside has a standard form we can factor later in this lesson.

The GCF for \(-18xy^2 + 27x^2y\) is \(-9xy\) (the leading coefficient is negative) and so:

\[ -18xy^2 + 27x^2y = -9xy(2y - 3x) \]

We have now learned the important first step in factoring polynomials: take out a GCF. But sometimes more has to be done. In the next section we will see how area models can help us factor polynomials further.

5.4.2 – Area Models and Basic Trinomials

Before we learn how to factor trinomials, it helps to understand how they are created when we multiply two binomials.
An area model organizes the multiplication so we can clearly see where each term comes from.

A closer look at multiplying binomials

Consider the product

\[ (x + 3)(x + 2). \]

An area model shows how each part multiplies:

\(x\) \(2\)
\(x\) \(x^2\) \(\textcolor{red}{2x}\)
\(3\) \(\textcolor{red}{3x}\) \(6\)

Combine the diagonal terms:

\[ x^2 + \textcolor{red}{2x} + \textcolor{red}{3x} + 6 = x^2 + \textcolor{red}{5x} + 6 \]

This reveals the pattern we will use when factoring:

  • The top-left box comes from multiplying the first terms
  • The bottom-right box comes from multiplying the last terms
  • The diagonal boxes combine to form the middle term

Factoring works by reversing this structure.

Using the area model to factor

To factor a trinomial like

\[ x^2 + 5x + 6, \]

we begin by placing the first and last terms where they belong:

? ?
? \(\textcolor{red}{x^2}\) ?
? ? \(\textcolor{red}{6}\)

The \(x^2\) term can only come from \(x \cdot x\), so we fill those in:

\(\textcolor{red}{x}\) ?
\(\textcolor{red}{x}\) \(x^2\) ?
? ? \(6\)

To determine the missing diagonal terms, notice that their coefficients must multiply to \(6\) (the constant term) and their sum must be \(5\) (the middle coefficient).

So we list the factor pairs of \(6\) and choose the pair whose sum is \(5\).

Factors Factor Sum
\(1 \times 6\) \(1 + 6 = 7\)
\(2 \times 3\) \(2 + 3 = 5\) ✓

Split the middle term using the factor pair we found:

\(x\) ?
\(x\) \(x^2\) \(\textcolor{red}{3x}\)
? \(\textcolor{red}{2x}\) \(6\)

Now the remaining entries fall into place:

\(x\) \(\textcolor{red}{3}\)
\(x\) \(x^2\) \(3x\)
\(\textcolor{red}{2}\) \(2x\) \(6\)

This gives us,

\[ x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 3)(x + 2) \]

Example 4: A positive constant and positive middle term

Factor \(x^2 + 11x + 24\).

We start by filling out what we know.

\(x\) ?
\(x\) \(x^2\) ?
? ? \(24\)

Now we look for factor pairs of \(24\) that add up to \(11\):

Factors Sum
\(1 \times 24\) \(25\)
\(2 \times 12\) \(14\)
\(3 \times 8\) \(11\) ✓
\(4 \times 6\) \(10\)

We use what we found to complete the area model.

\(x\) \(8\)
\(x\) \(x^2\) \(8x\)
\(3\) \(3x\) \(24\)

So,

\[ x^2 + 11x + 24 = (x + 3)(x + 8) \]

\[ x^2 + 4x + 4 = (x + 2)(x + 2) \]

Example 5: A negative constant with a negative middle term

Factor \(x^2 - 7x - 30\).

Fill in what we know.

\(x\) ?
\(x\) \(x^2\) ?
? ? \(-30\)

The last term is negative, so one factor is positive and one negative.
The middle term is negative, so the larger factor must be negative. We just need to find which factor pair add up to \(-7\).

Factors Sum
\(1 \times -30\) \(-29\)
\(2 \times -15\) \(-13\)
\(3 \times -10\) \(-7\) ✓

Now we can complete the area model.

\(x\) \(-10\)
\(x\) \(x^2\) \(-10x\)
\(3\) \(3x\) \(-30\)

Thus,

\[ x^2 - 7x - 30 = (x + 3)(x - 10) \]

Example 6: A negative constant with a positive middle term

Factor \(x^2 + 2x - 15\).

\(x\) ?
\(x\) \(x^2\) ?
? ? \(-15\)

Since the last term is negative, one factor must be positive and one negative.
The middle term is positive, so the larger factor is positive.

Factors Sum
\(-1 \times 15\) \(14\)
\(-3 \times 5\) \(2\) ✓
\(x\) \(-3\)
\(x\) \(x^2\) \(-3x\)
\(5\) \(5x\) \(-15\)

Thus,

\[ x^2 + 2x - 15 = (x + 5)(x - 3) \]

Since there is no middle term, it must be zero. We can rewrite this as:

\[ a^2 + 0a - 16 \]

So we are looking for numbers whose product is \(-16\) and whose sum is \(0\). The only pair that works is \(-4\) and \(4\).

\(a\) \(-4\)
\(a\) \(a^2\) \(-4a\)
\(4\) \(4a\) \(-16\)

So,

\[ a^2 - 16 = (a + 4)(a - 4) \]

We will see in the next section that this is a special form called a difference of squares.

Example 7: A positive constant with a negative middle term

Factor \(x^2 - 10x + 16\)

Fill in what we know.

\(x\) ?
\(x\) \(x^2\) ?
? ? \(16\)

The last term is positive, so both factors have the same sign.
The middle term is negative, so both should be negative.

Factors Sum
\(-1 \times -16\) \(-17\)
\(-2 \times -8\) \(-10\) ✓
\(-4 \times -4\) \(-8\)

Now complete the area model.

\(x\) \(-2\)
\(x\) \(x^2\) \(-2x\)
\(-8\) \(-8x\) \(16\)

Thus,

\[ x^2 - 10x + 16 = (x - 8)(x - 2) \]

For a trinomial \(x^2 + bx + c\):

  • If \(c\) is positive, both factors have the same sign
    – If \(b\) is positive → both positive
    – If \(b\) is negative → both negative

  • If \(c\) is negative, the factors have opposite signs
    – If \(b\) is positive → the larger factor is positive
    – If \(b\) is negative → the larger factor is negative

These patterns help you choose the correct factors quickly.

5.4.3 – Mixed Expressions (GCF and Area Model)

Sometimes a polynomial needs more than one step to be completely factored.
If every term has a common factor, we take out the GCF first, then use the area model to factor the remaining trinomial.

Taking out the GCF keeps the trinomial in its cleanest form and makes the area model easier to use.

Example 8: GCF first, then area model

Factor \(4x^2 + 20x + 24\).

Start by taking out the GCF:

\[ \text{GCF}(4x^2, 20x, 24) = 4 \]

\[ 4x^2 + 20x + 24 = 4(x^2 + 5x + 6) \]

Now factor the trinomial inside the parentheses.

We need coefficients that multiply to \(6\) and add to \(5\):

Factors Sum
\(1 \times 6\) \(7\)
\(2 \times 3\) \(5\) ✓

Fill the diagonal entries:

\(x\) \(3\)
\(x\) \(x^2\) \(3x\)
\(2\) \(2x\) \(6\)

So,

\[ x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3) \]

Final answer:

\[ 4x^2 + 20x + 24 = 4(x + 2)(x + 3) \]

If you factor out a GCF, don’t forget to include it in your answer!

Example 9: GCF fixes a negative leading coefficient

Factor \(-15x^2 + 45x - 30\).

Take out the negative GCF:

\[ \text{GCF}(-15x^2, 45x, -30) = -15 \]

\[ -15x^2 + 45x - 30 = -15(x^2 - 3x + 2) \]

Now factor \(x^2 - 3x + 2\).

We need factors of \(2\) that add to \(-3\):

Factors Sum
\(-1 \times -2\) \(-3\) ✓
\(x\) \(-1\)
\(x\) \(x^2\) \(-1x\)
\(-2\) \(-2x\) \(2\)

Thus,

\[ x^2 - 3x + 2 = (x - 1)(x - 2) \]

Final answer:

\[ -15x^2 + 45x - 30 = -15(x - 1)(x - 2) \]

Example 10: Polynomial written out of order

Factor \(-45x^2 - 5x^3 - 70x\).

Rewrite in standard form:

\[ -5x^3 - 45x^2 - 70x \]

Take out the GCF:

\[ \text{GCF}(-5x^3, -45x^2, -70x) = -5x \]

So,

\[ -5x^3 - 45x^2 - 70x = -5x(x^2 + 9x + 14) \]

Now to factor the trinomial. We need factors of \(14\) that add to \(9\):

Factors Sum
\(1 \times 14\) \(15\)
\(2 \times 7\) \(9\) ✓
\(x\) \(7\)
\(x\) \(x^2\) \(7x\)
\(2\) \(2x\) \(14\)

Thus,

\[ x^2 + 9x + 14 = (x + 2)(x + 7) \]

Final answer:

\[ -5x^3 - 45x^2 - 70x = -5x(x + 2)(x + 7) \]

Start by taking out a GCF \(-9b\).

\[ -9b^3 + 9b^2 + 18b = -9b(b^2 - b - 2) \]

Now factor the trinomial.

\(b\) \(1\)
\(b\) \(b^2\) \(b\)
\(-2\) \(-2b\) \(-2\)

So,

\[ -9b^3 + 9b^2 + 18b = -9b(b-2)(b+1) \]

  1. Put the polynomial in standard form
  2. Factor out a GCF if there is one
  3. If the first term is negative, include a negative sign with the GCF
  4. Use the area model to factor the trinomial
