5.1 - Exponent Rules

When we start working with algebraic expressions, we quickly see powers like \(x^2\), \(x^3\), or \(x^4\). These expressions use exponents to show repeated multiplication.

Instead of writing \(x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x\), we can write \(x^4\). This shorthand makes it much easier to work with larger problems, but it also means we need to know how powers behave when we multiply, divide, or raise them to other powers.

In this lesson, we’ll learn the rules, unpack why they work, and learn how to use them to simplify expressions.

base, exponent, negative exponent, positive exponent, zero exponent

5.1.1 - Understanding Powers

A power has two parts, a base and an exponent:

  • The base tells what number or variable is being multiplied.
  • The exponent tells how many times to multiply that base by itself.

For example, \(5^3 = 5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 = 125\). The base is 5. The exponent is 3. When two powers share the same base (like \(x^2\) and \(x^5\)) we call them like bases. That’s when these exponent rules apply.

Rule 1 - Product Rule

To understand how to multiplying powers with like bases works, let’s take an example and expand it out.

If we multiply \(x^3\) by \(x^5\), here’s what’s really happening:

\[ x^3 \cdot x^5 = (x \cdot x \cdot x) \cdot (x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x) \]

As you can see, we are really multiplying \(x\) times itself 8 times! That means we can simplify the process of multiplying by adding the exponents.

\[ x^3 \cdot x^5 = x^{3+5} = x^8 \]

In general, if you multiply powers with like bases, you add the exponents. This is called the product rule of exponents.

\[ a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n} \]

Example 1: A simple product

Simplify \(r^4 \cdot r^3\)

Using the product rule we get: \[ r^4 \cdot r^3 = r^{4+3} = r^7 \]

\(x^{9+3} = x^{12}\)

Rule 2 - Quotient Rule

When dividing powers with the same base, we’re really canceling out factors.

\[ \dfrac{x^7}{x^3} = \dfrac{x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x}{x \cdot x \cdot x} \]

Each \(x\) in the denominator cancels one \(x\) in the numerator. After canceling three, four remain:

\[ \dfrac{x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot \cancel{x} \cdot \cancel{x} \cdot \cancel{x}}{\cancel{x} \cdot \cancel{x} \cdot \cancel{x}} = x^4 \]

This is the same as subtracting the exponents.

\[ \dfrac{x^7}{x^3} = x^{7 - 3} = x^4 \]

In general, when you divide powers with like bases, you subtract the exponents. This is called the quotient rule of exponents.

\[ \dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n} \]

Example 2: A simple quotient

Simplify \(\dfrac{y^8}{y^5}\)

All we have to do is apply the quotient rule: \[ \dfrac{y^8}{y^5} = y^{8-5} = y^3 \]

\[ p^{9-4} = p^5 \]

Rule 3 - Power of a Power

What if we raise a power to another power?

\[ (x^3)^2 \]

Raising to the \(2^{\text{nd}}\) power means multiply by itself two times:

\[ \begin{aligned} (x^3)^2 &= (x^3) \cdot (x^3) \\ &= (x \cdot x \cdot x) \cdot (x \cdot x \cdot x) \\ &= x^{6} \end{aligned} \]

A simpler way to do this is to multiply the exponents.

\[ (x^3)^2 = x^(3 \times 2) = x^6 \]

In general, when you raise a power to a power you multiply the exponents.

\[ (a^m)^n = a^{m \cdot n} \]

Example 3: Powers of powers

Simplify \((m^2)^5\)

We just apply the rule \((x^a)^b = x^{a \cdot b}\).

\[ (m^2)^5 = m^{2 \times 5} = m^{10} \]

\[ x^{3 \times 2} = x^6 \]

Rule 4 - Power of a Product

When an exponent applies to a product, it applies to each factor.

\[ \begin{aligned} (ab)^3 &= (ab)\cdot (ab) \cdot (ab) \\ &= (a \cdot a \cdot a) \cdot (b \cdot b \cdot b) \\ &= a^3 b^3 \end{aligned} \]

This is an extension of the “power of a power” idea to two factors.

\[ (ab)^n = a^n b^n \]

Example 4: Distributing exponents

Simplify \((2x)^4\)

All we have to do is apply the rule \((ab)^n = a^n b^n\). \[ (2x)^4 = 2^4 x^4 = 16x^4 \]

\[ (3y)^3 = 3^3 y^3 = 27y^3 \]

Rule 5 - Power of a Quotient

The same idea works for fractions, both numerator and denominator get the exponent.

\[ \begin{aligned} \left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right)^3 &= \left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right) \cdot \left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right)\cdot \left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right) \\ &= \dfrac{x \cdot x \cdot x}{y \cdot y \cdot y} \\ &= \dfrac{x^3}{y^3} \end{aligned} \]

In general:

\[ \left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^n = \dfrac{a^n}{b^n} \]

Example 5: Distributing exponents of a fraction

Simplify \(\left(\dfrac{x}{3}\right)^2\)

\[ \left(\dfrac{x}{3}\right)^2 = \dfrac{x^2}{9} \]

\[ \dfrac{8m^3}{125} \]

Rule 6 - Zero Exponent

A zero exponent has a special property: anything raised to the zero power is \(1\) (as long as the base is not \(0\)).

\[ a^0 = 1 \quad (a \ne 0) \]

Example 6: The power of zero

Simplify \((5y)^0\)

\[ (5y)^0 = 1 \]

\[ 1 \]

You may see \(0^0\) treated differently in different contexts. Some mathematicians prefer to think of \(0^0\) as undefined or indeterminate. This is common in courses such as Calculus.

For consistency, in Algebra, we will use the definition:

\[ 0^0 = 1 \]

Rule 7 - Negative Exponent

Idea: A negative exponent counts how many factors you still need in the denominator. Start from the quotient rule:

\[ \dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n} \]

If \(m<n\), then \(m-n\) is negative. But canceling shows what it means:

\[ \dfrac{a^3}{a^5} = \dfrac{1}{a^2} = a^{-2} \]

So a negative exponent just moves the factor to the denominator (or to the numerator if it’s already in the denominator):

\[ a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}, \qquad \dfrac{1}{a^{-n}} = a^n \qquad (a\neq 0) \]

First move factors with negative exponents to the other side of the fraction bar, then apply other rules.

For example:

\[ \begin{aligned} \dfrac{x^{-2} \cdot y^3}{x^{-3} \cdot y^{-2}} &= \dfrac{x^3 \cdot y^3 \cdot y^2}{x^2} \\ &= \dfrac{x^3 y^5}{x^2} \\ &= x^{3-2} y^5 \\ &= xy^5 \end{aligned} \]

Example 7: Negative exponents

Simplify \(\dfrac{x^{-4}y}{x^2y^{-2}}\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \dfrac{x^{-4}y}{x^2y^{-2}} &= \dfrac{y \cdot y^2}{x^4 \cdot x^2} \\ &= \dfrac{y^{1 + 2}}{x^{4 + 2}} \\ &= \dfrac{y^3}{x^6} \end{aligned} \]

\[ \dfrac{3}{6},x^{-7},y^4=\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{y^4}{x^7}=\dfrac{y^4}{2x^7} \]

Rule 8 - Fractional Exponents (optional)

Idea: Fractional exponents connect powers and roots. The denominator of the fraction is the root, and the numerator is the power.

Start with the unit fraction: \[ a^{\tfrac{1}{n}}=\sqrt[n]{a}\quad (a\ge 0 \text{ for real roots}) \]

Then combine with a power:

\[ a^{\tfrac{m}{n}} = \big(a^{\tfrac{1}{n}}\big)^m = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m = \sqrt[n]{a^m} \]

Example 8: Exponents and roots

Simplify \(8^{\tfrac{2}{3}}\)

\[ \sqrt[3]{8^2} = \sqrt[3]{64} = 4 \]

\[ \left(\sqrt[4]{16}\right)^3 = 2^3 = 8 \]

\[ \sqrt[5]{x^2}=x^{\tfrac{2}{5}} \quad\text{and}\quad \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{y^4}}=y^{-\tfrac{4}{3}} \]

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • \((-3)^2=9\), but \(-3^2=-9\). The negative must be inside parentheses to be part of the base.
  • Exponents distribute over multiplication/division, not over addition/subtraction: \((x+y)^2\ne x^2+y^2\).
  • A negative exponent doesn’t make the number negative, it moves the base to the other side of the fraction bar.
  • \(a^0=1\) not 0.

5.1.2 - Combining Rules

Now that you know all the rules, it’s time to put them to use on more complex expressions. A helpful routine:

  1. Inside first: simplify inside parentheses.
  2. Exponents next: handle powers like \((\cdot)^n\).
  3. Then multiply/divide: combine like bases with product/quotient rules.
  4. Finish clean: write with positive exponents.

Example 9: Multiple rules

Simplify \(\dfrac{(2x^3)^2}{x^5}\)

First simplify the numerator. \[ (2x^3)^2=4x^6 \]

Now apply the quotient rule:

\[ \dfrac{4x^6}{x^5}=4x^{6-5}=4x \]

Example 10: Getting tricky

Simplify \((3a^2b^{-3})^{-2}\)

First, apply the outer exponent using the rule \((x^a)^b = x^{a \cdot b}\)

\[ (3a^2b^{-3})^{-2} = 3^{-2}a^{-4}b^6 \]

Finally, rewrite it so that all of the exponents are positive:

\[ 3^{-2}a^{-4}b^6 = \dfrac{b^6}{9a^4} \]

Example 11: A mess of exponents

Simplify \(\dfrac{(x^{-2}y^4)^3}{x^3y^{-2}}\)

First, apply the outer exponent using the power of a power rule:

\[ \dfrac{(x^{-2}y^4)^3}{x^3y^{-2}} = \dfrac{x^{-6}y^{12}}{x^3y^{-2}} \]

Now apply the quotient rule:

\[ \dfrac{x^{-6}y^{12}}{x^3y^{-2}} = x^{-6 - 3}y^{12 - (-2)} = x^{-9}y^{14} \]

Finally, rewrite it so that the exponents are all positive:

\[ x^{-9}y^{14} = \dfrac{y^{14}}{x^9} \]

Rule Algebraic Rule Example
Product Rule \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m + n}\) \(x^4 \cdot x^3 = x^7\)
Quotient Rule \(\dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m - n}\) \(\dfrac{y^8}{y^2} = y^6\)
Power of a Power \((a^m)^n = a^{m \cdot n}\) \((x^3)^2 = x^6\)
Power of a Product \((a \cdot b)^n = a^n \cdot b^n\) \((3b)^2 = 9b^2\)
Power of a Quotient \(\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^n = \dfrac{a^n}{b^n}\) \(\left(\dfrac{x}{2}\right)^3 = \dfrac{x^3}{8}\)
Zero Exponent \(a^0 = 1\) \(m^0 = 1\)
Negative Exponent \(a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}\) \(k^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{k^2}\)
Fractional Exponent \(a^{\tfrac{m}{n}}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}\) \(x^{\tfrac{2}{3}}=\sqrt[3]{x^2}\)
